Module 1

Construction, Working Principle, Types and Applications

Start here: what a D.C. motor is made of, why it turns, and which type suits which job.

1.1

Introduction to D.C. motors

A D.C. motor is a rotating electrical machine that converts electrical energy taken from a direct-current (D.C.) supply into mechanical energy in the form of a rotating shaft. The shaft then drives a load such as a fan, pump, conveyor belt or the wheels of a vehicle.

Electrical input

Voltage V (volts) and current I (amperes) taken from the D.C. supply.

Input power = V × I watts.

Mechanical output

Torque T (N·m) at the shaft, turning at speed N (rpm).

Output power = 2πNT / 60 watts.

Not all of the input becomes useful output. Some energy is lost as heat in the windings (copper loss), in the iron core (iron loss), and as friction in bearings and brushes. That is why output power is always less than input power, and efficiency is always below 100%.

Where you meet D.C. motors

Toy cars and cooling fans in computers, car windscreen wipers and power windows, cordless drills, e-rickshaws and battery forklifts, cranes, hoists, rolling mills and paper machines. They are chosen when we need high starting torque or easy, smooth speed control.

1.2

Construction of a D.C. motor

A D.C. motor has two main parts. The stator is the stationary part: yoke, poles and field winding. It produces the magnetic field. The rotor (armature) is the rotating part: armature core, armature winding, commutator and shaft. It carries the current that experiences the force. Brushes and bearings connect the two worlds: brushes carry current into the rotating armature, and bearings let the shaft turn smoothly.

1.3

Working principle of a D.C. motor

Motor principle. When a current-carrying conductor is placed in a magnetic field, it experiences a mechanical force. The size of the force is F = BIL newtons, where B is flux density (Wb/m², tesla), I is current (A) and L is the length of conductor inside the field (m).

Why is there a force? The current in the conductor makes its own circular magnetic field. On one side of the conductor this field adds to the main field (the field becomes stronger); on the other side it opposes the main field (the field becomes weaker). Magnetic lines of force behave like stretched rubber bands, so the conductor is pushed from the stronger-field side towards the weaker-field side.

Fleming’s Left-Hand Rule

Stretch the thumb, first finger and second finger of your left hand so that they are at right angles to each other.

  • First finger points in the direction of the magnetic Field (from N to S).
  • Second finger points in the direction of Current.
  • Thumb gives the direction of force, that is, Motion of the conductor.

From force on one conductor to rotation

Place a rectangular coil (two conductors joined at the back) between the poles and free to turn on a shaft. The two sides of the coil carry current in opposite directions, so the forces on them are opposite: one side is pushed up and the other down. These two equal and opposite forces form a couple, and a couple produces torque that turns the coil.

A real armature has many coils placed in slots all round the core, so at every instant many conductors are under the poles and producing force. Their torques add up to give smooth, continuous rotation.

Why the commutator is essential

As the coil passes the vertical position (the magnetic neutral axis), each side moves from under one pole to under the other. If the current in the coil stayed the same, the force would now act the other way and push the coil back. The commutator, together with the brushes, reverses the current in the coil at exactly that moment. So the conductor under the N pole always carries current in the same direction, and the torque stays in one direction: unidirectional torque.

1.4

Types of D.C. motors

D.C. motors are classified by how the field winding gets its current, that is, how the field winding is connected to the armature and the supply.

Connection diagrams

Understand

Shunt, series and compound: the key difference

Shunt motor. The field winding has many turns of thin wire and is connected in parallel with the armature. Its current is small and nearly constant, so the flux is nearly constant. Result: speed falls only slightly as load rises, so it is called a nearly constant-speed motor.

Series motor. The field winding has a few turns of thick wire and is connected in series with the armature, so the same current flows through both. Flux rises with load current, so torque rises roughly as the square of current (T ∝ Ia² before the iron saturates). Result: very high starting torque, but speed falls sharply with load and rises dangerously at no load. A series motor must never be started or run without load.

Compound motor. It has both a shunt field and a series field. In the commonly used cumulative compound motor the two fields help each other, giving a combination of good starting torque and a definite no-load speed.

Permanent magnet D.C. (PMDC) motors, found in toys, wipers and cordless tools, use permanent magnets instead of a field winding. They behave like a separately excited motor with fixed flux.

1.5

Applications of D.C. motors

A motor is chosen by matching its torque–speed behaviour to what the load needs. Heavy loads that must be started from rest need high starting torque. Machine tools need steady speed. Lifts and process lines need smooth control.

Module 2

Back EMF and Torque Equation

Two equations describe what happens inside a running motor. You will state and use them; no derivations are needed.

2.1

Back EMF

When the armature rotates, its conductors cut the magnetic flux of the field poles. By Faraday’s law of electromagnetic induction, an EMF is induced in them, exactly as in a D.C. generator. So a running motor is also acting as a generator at the same time.

By Lenz’s law, an induced EMF always opposes the cause that produces it. Here the cause is the current driven by the supply, so the induced EMF acts against the supply voltage. It is therefore called the back EMF or counter EMF, written Eb.

The armature circuit then has the supply voltage pushing current one way and the back EMF pushing the other way. The net voltage, V − Eb, drives the armature current through the armature resistance:

V = Eb + IaRa    or    Ia = V − EbRa
V
supply voltage across the armature, volts (V)
Eb
back EMF, volts (V)
Ia
armature current, amperes (A)
Ra
armature resistance, ohms (Ω)

This simplified equation neglects the small voltage drop at the brushes (about 1 to 2 V in total). When the brush drop is given, write V = Eb + IaRa + Vbrush.

Why back EMF is important

1. It limits the armature current. Because Ia = (V − Eb)/Ra and Ra is very small (often below 1 Ω), only a small net voltage is needed to drive rated current. Back EMF uses up most of the supply voltage and keeps current safe.

2. It makes the motor self-regulating. If the load increases, the motor slows slightly, so Eb falls. Then V − Eb rises, armature current rises, and the motor develops more torque to carry the extra load. If the load falls, the opposite happens. The motor automatically draws just the current its load needs.

3. It represents the useful power. The power converted from electrical to mechanical form is Pm = EbIa watts.

At the starting instant, the armature is at rest, so N = 0 and Eb = 0. The only thing limiting current is the tiny armature resistance:

Ia(start) = V / Ra. For a 220 V motor with Ra = 0.5 Ω this is 220 / 0.5 = 440 A, while the rated current may be only 10 to 20 A.

Such a current can burn the armature winding, damage the commutator with heavy sparking, and blow fuses. So a starter (for example a 3-point or 4-point starter) adds resistance in series with the armature at starting and cuts it out step by step as speed and back EMF build up.

Worked example

Apply

A 220 V D.C. shunt motor has armature resistance 0.5 Ω and draws an armature current of 20 A. Find the back EMF and the starting current if no starter is used.

Given: V = 220 V, Ra = 0.5 Ω, Ia = 20 A

Back EMF: Eb = V − IaRa = 220 − (20 × 0.5) = 220 − 10 = 210 V

Starting current (Eb = 0): Ia = V / Ra = 220 / 0.5 = 440 A, which is 22 times the running current.

2.2

Back EMF equation

The back EMF has the same form as the EMF equation of a D.C. generator. It is stated here without derivation:

Eb = P Φ Z N60 A  volts
Eb
back EMF, volts (V)
P
number of poles (a pure number, always even: 2, 4, 6…)
Φ
magnetic flux per pole, webers (Wb)
Z
total number of armature conductors
N
armature speed, revolutions per minute (rpm)
A
number of parallel paths in the armature winding: A = P for lap winding, A = 2 for wave winding

The number 60 converts speed from revolutions per minute into revolutions per second.

What the equation tells us

For a given motor, P, Z and A are fixed by its design. So we can write Eb ∝ ΦN:

  • With flux constant, back EMF is directly proportional to speed. Double the speed, double the back EMF.
  • With speed constant, back EMF is directly proportional to flux. A weaker field gives less back EMF at the same speed.
  • Rearranging gives N ∝ Eb / Φ, which is the basis of speed control in Module 3.

Worked example

Apply

A 4-pole, lap-wound D.C. motor has 480 armature conductors and a flux of 0.02 Wb per pole. Find the back EMF when it runs at 1000 rpm.

Given: P = 4, Z = 480, Φ = 0.02 Wb, N = 1000 rpm, lap winding so A = P = 4

Formula: Eb = PΦZN / (60A)

Substitute: Eb = (4 × 0.02 × 480 × 1000) / (60 × 4) = 38 400 / 240

Answer: Eb = 160 V

2.3

Torque equation of a D.C. motor

Torque is the turning effect of a force about an axis. It equals force multiplied by the perpendicular distance from the axis: T = F × r, measured in newton-metres (N·m). In a motor, the forces on all the armature conductors act at the radius of the armature and together produce the electromagnetic torque that turns the shaft.

Stated without derivation, the torque developed in the armature is:

T = P Z2π A Φ Ia  N·m    ≈   0.159 P ZA Φ Ia
T
electromagnetic (armature) torque, newton-metres (N·m)
P
number of poles
Z
total number of armature conductors
Φ
flux per pole, webers (Wb)
Ia
armature current, amperes (A)
A
number of parallel paths

For a given motor, P, Z and A are constant, so:

T ∝ Φ Ia
  • Shunt motor: flux is nearly constant, so T ∝ Ia. A straight-line relationship.
  • Series motor: flux is produced by the armature current itself, so Φ ∝ Ia (before saturation) and T ∝ Ia². This is why a series motor gives very high starting torque.

Electromagnetic torque vs. shaft torque. The torque in the equation is the electromagnetic (gross) torque developed in the armature. Part of it is used up in overcoming iron loss and mechanical losses (friction at bearings and brushes, air resistance). The torque actually available at the shaft is the shaft (useful) torque Tsh, which is always a little less:

Tsh = T − Tlost   and   output power = 2πNTsh / 60

Worked example

Apply

The same 4-pole, lap-wound motor (Z = 480, Φ = 0.02 Wb) carries an armature current of 50 A. Find the electromagnetic torque.

Given: P = 4, Z = 480, A = 4, Φ = 0.02 Wb, Ia = 50 A

Formula: T = (PZ / 2πA) × Φ × Ia

Step 1: PZ / 2πA = (4 × 480) / (2 × 3.1416 × 4) = 1920 / 25.133 = 76.39

Step 2: T = 76.39 × 0.02 × 50 = 76.39 × 1.0

Answer: T ≈ 76.4 N·m (electromagnetic torque). The shaft torque will be somewhat less because of losses.

Module 3

Speed Control of D.C. Shunt Motor

Three practical ways to make a shunt motor run faster or slower, and how to choose between them.

3.1

Introduction to speed control

Speed control means deliberately changing the speed of a motor, while it carries its load, to suit the job. Industry needs this constantly:

  • a lathe turns slowly for a large diameter workpiece and fast for a small one;
  • a lift must start smoothly, run at a steady speed and slow down before stopping;
  • rolling mills, paper mills and textile machines must match the speeds of several rollers;
  • conveyors and pumps change speed to change the rate of production or flow.

Where the speed relation comes from

From Module 2, Eb = V − IaRa, and for a given motor Eb ∝ ΦN. Putting them together:

N ∝ EbΦ = V − IaRaΦ

This is an approximate, steady-state relationship for a motor of fixed construction (fixed P, Z, A). It shows exactly three “handles” for changing speed:

What we changeMethodEffect on speed
Flux ΦField (flux) controlLess flux gives more speed (above normal speed)
Resistance in armature circuitArmature resistance (rheostatic) controlMore resistance gives less speed (below normal speed)
Voltage V across armatureArmature voltage controlLess voltage gives less speed (below normal speed)

For comparing two operating conditions of the same motor, the useful working form is:

N2N1 = Eb2Eb1 × Φ1Φ2
3.2

Field (flux) control method

A variable resistance called the shunt field regulator (field rheostat) is connected in series with the shunt field winding. Increasing this resistance reduces the field current Ish = V / (Rsh + Rf), which reduces the flux Φ.

Because N ∝ Eb/Φ, a weaker field makes the motor run faster. Here is why physically: when flux falls, back EMF falls at once, armature current rises sharply, torque rises above the load torque, and the motor accelerates until back EMF builds up again to balance the supply.

This is called field weakening. With the regulator at zero, the motor runs at its normal (rated) speed. Adding resistance can only reduce flux, so this method gives speeds above normal speed only.

Simulation: field control

Apply

Practical limits. Very weak fields cause unstable running and heavy sparking at the commutator (poor commutation), and the higher armature current at the same load may overheat the armature. Mechanical stress on the rotor rises with speed. In practice field control is used up to about 2 to 3 times normal speed.

Never open the field circuit of a running shunt motor. Flux would collapse to a small residual value and the motor could race to a dangerously high speed. Field-failure protection (the no-volt coil of a 4-point or 3-point starter) guards against this.

Worked example

Apply

A 220 V shunt motor has Ra = 0.5 Ω. It runs at 1000 rpm taking an armature current of 20 A. The field is weakened so that flux becomes 80% of its original value, and the armature current rises to 25 A. Find the new speed.

Eb1 = 220 − 20 × 0.5 = 210 V    Eb2 = 220 − 25 × 0.5 = 207.5 V

N2 = N1 × (Eb2/Eb1) × (Φ1/Φ2) = 1000 × (207.5/210) × (1/0.8) = 1000 × 0.988 × 1.25

N2 ≈ 1235 rpm. Reducing flux by 20% raised the speed by about 23%.

3.3

Armature resistance control method

A variable resistance Rext, called the armature controller, is connected in series with the armature. The shunt field stays connected directly across the supply, so the flux does not change.

The full armature current flows through Rext and drops a voltage IaRext across it. Less voltage is left for the armature, so back EMF and speed fall:

N ∝ V − Ia(Ra + Rext)   (Φ constant)

Increasing Rext therefore gives speeds below normal speed. Current path: supply positive terminal → armature controller → armature → supply negative terminal; the field current takes its own parallel path through the shunt field.

Simulation: armature resistance control

Apply

Limitations. (1) Large power loss Ia²Rext in the controller, so efficiency is poor, especially at low speed. (2) Poor speed regulation: because the drop IaRext depends on load, speed changes a lot when the load changes. (3) The controller must carry full armature current, so it is bulky and needs cooling. (4) Speeds only below normal.

Worked example

Apply

The same motor (220 V, Ra = 0.5 Ω, 1000 rpm at Ia = 20 A). A resistance of 4.5 Ω is added in series with the armature. The load torque is unchanged, so Ia stays 20 A. Find the new speed and the loss in the resistance.

Eb2 = 220 − 20 × (0.5 + 4.5) = 220 − 100 = 120 V

Flux is constant, so N2 = 1000 × (120/210) = 571 rpm

Loss in the added resistance = 20² × 4.5 = 1800 W, out of an armature input of 220 × 20 = 4400 W. About 41% of the input is wasted as heat.

3.4

Armature voltage control method

The field is kept excited from a separate, constant D.C. supply so that flux stays constant, and the voltage applied to the armature is varied. With Φ constant:

N ∝ V − IaRa   (Φ constant)

Since IaRa is small, speed is almost proportional to armature voltage. Lower voltage gives lower speed; raising the voltage back to rated voltage gives rated speed. Speed can be varied smoothly from near zero up to normal speed.

The variable D.C. voltage can come from:

  • a controlled rectifier (thyristor converter) fed from the A.C. mains, the most common method today;
  • a D.C. chopper when the source is a battery, as in battery vehicles;
  • the older Ward-Leonard system, where a motor-driven D.C. generator with an adjustable field supplies the armature.

Simulation: armature voltage control

Apply

Safe operation. Armature voltage must not exceed rated voltage, and the controller should limit armature current to a safe value during starting and acceleration. Because the field is kept at full strength, the motor can deliver rated torque at every speed (a constant-torque drive).

Worked example

Apply

The same motor (Ra = 0.5 Ω, 1000 rpm at 220 V and Ia = 20 A) has its armature voltage reduced to 110 V with field current held constant. Load torque is unchanged. Find the new speed.

Φ and load torque constant, so Ia stays 20 A. Eb2 = 110 − 20 × 0.5 = 100 V

N2 = 1000 × (100/210) = 476 rpm. No resistor wastes power here; only the small armature copper loss remains.

Revision sheet

  • A D.C. motor converts electrical energy into mechanical energy. It works on the motor principle: a current-carrying conductor in a magnetic field experiences a force F = BIL.
  • Direction of force: Fleming’s Left-Hand Rule (First finger = Field, seCond finger = Current, thuMb = Motion).
  • Stator: yoke, poles (core and shoe), field winding. Rotor: armature core, armature winding, commutator, shaft. Brushes carry current to the commutator; bearings support the shaft.
  • The commutator reverses current in each coil as it passes the neutral axis, so torque stays unidirectional.
  • Types: separately excited; self-excited shunt, series, compound. Shunt is nearly constant-speed; series has very high starting torque and must never run without load; compound combines both.
  • Back EMF is induced in the rotating armature and opposes the supply (Lenz’s law). V = Eb + IaRa. It limits current and makes the motor self-regulating.
  • At starting Eb = 0, so Ia = V/Ra is very large; a starter adds series resistance to limit it.
  • Eb = PΦZN/60A so Eb ∝ ΦN.   T = (PZ/2πA)ΦIa so T ∝ ΦIa. Shaft torque is less than electromagnetic torque.
  • Speed: N ∝ (V − IaRa)/Φ. Field control gives speeds above normal; armature resistance and armature voltage control give speeds below normal.
